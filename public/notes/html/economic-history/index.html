Orientation

What problem is Beckert trying to solve?

Beckert asks how cotton moved from a geographically dispersed, household-based industry led by Asia into a European-centered, factory-based global system - and why this transformation became a decisive engine of modern capitalism.

Central claimIndustrial capitalism did not arise inside an autonomous European economy. It grew from a prior regime of imperial expansion, armed trade, expropriation, slavery, and state power that Beckert calls war capitalism. Mechanized factories and wage labor at the core remained dependent on violently mobilized land and labor at the periphery.
直觉不要把 Industrial Revolution 想成 Manchester 工厂内部突然“发明”出来的故事。把镜头拉远：机器在英国，原料在美洲，消费者遍及非洲与全球，控制连接的是国家、商人、殖民权力与奴隶制。

Week 5 · Coercion Connection

How this reading functions in the week's narrative

Week-level questionWas coercion merely a morally abhorrent feature surrounding capitalist development, or was it a causal component of how modern industrial capitalism emerged and scaled?

Beckert supplies the week's system-building narrative. Chapters 1-2 move from an Asian-led, polycentric cotton economy to European control of trade through empire and armed commerce (pp. 3-55). Chapters 3-5 connect that network power to British mechanization and then to the Caribbean and U.S. slave-cotton frontiers (pp. 56-135). Chapter 6 asks why the factory system spread unevenly and makes state capacity and political coalitions decisive (pp. 136-174).

Olmstead and Rhode serve as the week's evidence and causal-identification audit. They accept that slavery was brutal, profitable, and globally connected, but challenge four stronger propositions: that slave cotton was necessary for industrialization, that enslaved labor was economically “cheap,” that cotton capitalists coordinated state policy, and that intensifying torture caused productivity growth (Olmstead & Rhode 2018, pp. 1-15).

Read Beckert forUse Olmstead-Rhode to test
Network formation, empire, externalization, and coexistence of slavery with wage labor.Whether dependence proves necessity and whether counterfactual free-labor supply was feasible.
State-supported reorganization of markets, land, and labor.Whether “cotton capitalists” were unified and whether the cited policies actually occurred.
Plantation violence and labor control as part of industrial scale.Whether violence explains the time trend in productivity once seeds, gins, transport, and selection are included.
Cotton's systemic centrality.Whether export importance is being confused with GDP magnitude or national-growth indispensability.

Master argument

The causal chain

Asian-centered cotton worlds → European armed trade and conquest → slavery + land expropriation + protected markets → British capital, cotton, technology, and export demand → mechanization → exploding raw-cotton demand → U.S. slave frontier → selective industrialization under strong states

Four propositions

  1. Europe's initial advantage was political-military, not technological. Europeans captured routes, markets, land, and labor before gaining a productivity lead.
  2. Capitalism changes form. War capitalism uses conquest, slavery, private coercion, and weak frontier restraints; industrial capitalism uses factories, wage labor, contracts, bureaucratic states, and national institutions.
  3. The forms coexist. Lancashire wage labor and Mississippi slavery were mutually dependent. From roughly 1770 to 1860 industrial capitalism intensified war capitalism.
  4. State power is constitutive. Markets are made through tariffs, navies, legal rules, infrastructure, fiscal capacity, labor law, and border control.
Epistemic cautionBeckert often moves from evidence that coercion and empire were historically central to stronger language of necessity. The first is well supported; the second is a counterfactual claim. Olmstead and Rhode attack exactly this move.
Chapter 1 · pp. 3-28

The Rise of a Global Commodity

Chapter thesisFor millennia cotton production was polycentric, household-based, technologically sophisticated, and mainly local or regional. India led manufacturing; Europe was peripheral. European ascent was not the natural victory of a long-superior industry.

1. Cotton was global before globalization was European [pp. 3-14]

Cotton was independently domesticated and processed across South Asia, the Americas, and Africa. Its climate band ran roughly from 32-35° south to 37° north. Four domesticated species mattered: G. hirsutum (Mesoamerican), G. barbadense (South American), G. herbaceum (African), and G. arboreum (Asian). Human selection transformed perennial shrubs into compact plants with longer, whiter, more abundant lint.

  • South Asia: cottonseed at Mehrgarh around 5000 BCE; textiles at Mohenjo-Daro, 3250-2750 BCE.
  • Peru: cotton fishing nets around 2400 BCE and textiles around 1600-1500 BCE.
  • Mesoamerica: cotton sustained tribute, clothing, exchange, ritual, and hierarchy. Beckert estimates pre-Columbian Mexico produced 116 million pounds annually, comparable to the U.S. crop in 1816 (p. 9).
  • Africa: cotton spread from Nubia/Egypt and expanded westward with Islamic skills and dress norms.
直觉欧洲后来进入的是一个已经运行数千年、技术与审美都高度成熟的 Asian/African/American world。

2. India was the center [pp. 7-18]

From antiquity into the nineteenth century, India led cotton manufacture. Peasant households combined food production with cotton growing, spinning, and weaving. They mastered roller gins, bows, spinning wheels, treadle looms, dyes, printing, and fine muslins. Merchant capital linked rural producers through putting-out networks. Producers often owned tools and controlled rhythm and disposal, though merchant power varied.

3. Markets expanded without destroying the household [pp. 18-22]

Trade encouraged workshops and specialized male weaving, but production stayed rural and domestic. Technology improved gradually; before the late eighteenth century output grew mainly through more households and more labor time, not radical productivity.

4. Europe's first industries remained dependent [pp. 22-28]

Northern Italy developed manufacture from the late twelfth century using wool skills, merchant capital, Islamic technology, and Levantine cotton. Milan employed about 6,000 cotton workers by 1450. Southern Germany exploited cheaper, less guild-regulated rural labor; Ulm connected about 2,000 urban and 18,000 rural workers.

Mechanism evidenceThe Asian spinning wheel roughly tripled productivity; the treadle loom freed both hands and enabled finer cloth (p. 25). Yet Italian and German industries did not control cotton growers or routes. Ottoman consolidation exposed that vulnerability.

The old world was discontinuous, multifocal, and relatively horizontal. Beckert's closing lesson: lasting success required power over supply networks, not simple participation in exchange.

Chapter 2 · pp. 29-55

Building War Capitalism

Chapter thesisBetween 1500 and 1780, Europeans became central through armed trade, imperial conquest, chartered companies, slavery, expropriation, protectionism, and appropriation of Asian knowledge, not superior production.

1. Definition [pp. 29-31, 38-39]

War capitalism is the outward-facing regime in which European capital operated beyond restraints applying at home. It combined armed commerce, fiscal-military states, conquest, enslavement, land seizure, monopoly companies, insurance and credit, protected markets, and industrial espionage.

直觉“war” 不只是国家战争，而是资本家能在海外以暴力重写产权、劳动与贸易规则；inside Europe 与 outside Europe 使用两套制度。

2. Columbus and da Gama [pp. 31-35]

Columbus (1492) enabled Atlantic seizure; da Gama (1497-98) provided direct maritime access to Indian textiles. Companies used ships, forts, and monopoly privileges. European power in India was initially shallow: factories depended on banias, weavers, bankers, and rulers. Political intervention gradually shifted power.

Scale evidenceThe EIC imported roughly 50,000 pieces into Britain in 1621 and five times that forty years later; cotton was over 75 percent of EIC exports by 1766. British untailored-piece exports rose from 12,500 (1614) to about 877,789 annually (1699-1701). European cloth exports from India rose from 30 million yards (1727) to 80 million (1790s), pp. 33, 44-46.

3. A four-continent web [pp. 35-47]

Indian cottons served Europe, Southeast Asian trade, and West African exchange for captives. Enslaved people were sent to plantations; metals and plantation goods financed Asian purchases. More than 8 million enslaved people crossed the Atlantic in the three centuries after 1500, over 5 million in the eighteenth century. In Richard Miles's 1,308 Gold Coast barters for 2,218 captives, textiles were over half the value; woven goods were nearly 60 percent of imports into Luanda (p. 36). African consumers demanded precise goods, forcing imitation of Indian designs.

4. Protection, knowledge, capital [pp. 47-55]

British Calico Acts, French prohibitions, and restrictions elsewhere sheltered domestic producers. Europeans could exclude Indian cloth at home while using it in imperial trade. Observers copied Indian dyeing, printing, weaving, and design. Atlantic merchants accumulated capital and expertise in credit, insurance, shipping, risk, and accounting. Britain exported about one-third of cotton output in 1760 and about two-thirds by century's end; at mid-century 94 percent of exports went to Africa and the Americas (p. 51). By 1780 Europe lacked a production lead but dominated transoceanic connections.

Chapter 3 · pp. 56-82

The Wages of War Capitalism

Chapter thesisBritish mechanization was a local breakthrough embedded in global structures. High wages and global opportunities induced labor-saving innovation; imperial supply, capital, markets, and state capacity made it viable.

1. Samuel Greg as microcosm [pp. 56-63]

Quarry Bank Mill (1784) joined water frames, Caribbean cotton, child apprentices, adult wage workers, merchant finance, and global sales. Greg's family linked textiles, Atlantic commerce, plantations, and slavery. His £3,000 investment reportedly returned 18 percent annually, four times government bonds (p. 62). Beckert does not say every manufacturer traded slaves; all operated in a system shaped by slavery and empire.

2. Why Britain? [pp. 63-65]

Markets, waterways, inventors, coal, dissent, and institutions mattered but were not unique. Britain's distinction was global network control. Around 1770 Lancashire wages may have been six times Indian wages, while productivity was only two to three times higher. Competition demanded a step-change.

3. Innovation sequence [pp. 65-69]

DateInnovationEffect
1733Kay's flying shuttleRoughly doubled weaving productivity; created yarn shortage.
1760sHargreaves's jennyMultiple spindles; tripled speed by 1767; about 20,000 by 1786.
1769Arkwright's water frameContinuous strong yarn; concentrated powered production.

ProductivityWith Roberts's automated mule, a yarn task took only 135 hours; across three decades productivity increased roughly 370-fold (pp. 66-67). British yarn became cheaper than Indian yarn.

4. Demand and deindustrialization [pp. 69-76]

Cotton was 56 percent of increased British exports between 1784-86 and 1804-06. Bengal piece-goods exports to Britain fell from £1.4 million (1800-01) to just over £330,000 (1809-10), pp. 74-75. Beckert emphasizes colonial rule, protection, naval power, and unequal access, not comparative advantage alone.

5. New institutional form and coexistence [pp. 76-82]

Industrial capitalism mobilized wage labor, property, infrastructure, capital, and demand through a bureaucratic state enforcing contracts, borders, taxes, and markets. British state revenue grew about sixteenfold; customs gave roughly one-third of 1800 tax revenue. Commerce funded naval power, which protected commerce. Factory demand simultaneously intensified coerced cotton abroad.

逻辑转折核心是 wage labor + contract + strong state；但核心可以把最暴力的 land/labor mobilization externalize 到海外。
Chapter 4 · pp. 83-97

Capturing Labor, Conquering Land

Chapter thesisMechanization created a raw-cotton demand shock older peasant systems could not rapidly satisfy. Caribbean and Brazilian slave plantations supplied elastic land and labor, creating “second slavery.”

1. Bottleneck [pp. 83-88]

Britain spun 5.1 million pounds in 1781; this multiplied sixfold by 1790 and reached 56 million pounds by 1800. Ottoman subsistence, labor scarcity, local demand, infrastructure, and elites constrained supply; its British share fell from about 20 percent (1786-90) to 1.28 percent twenty years later and 0.29 percent a decade after. India faced EIC textile interests, food-security fears, transport cost, and short staple; African schemes failed to scale.

2. Caribbean elasticity [pp. 88-93]

Planters controlled land, credit, ports, and enslaved labor. British West Indian imports quadrupled in 1781-91; Barbados exports rose from 240,000 pounds (1768) to 2.6 million (1789). Saint-Domingue exported 6.8 million pounds to France in 1791, supported by roughly a quarter-million forced African arrivals in 1784-91.

Timing46 percent of all people enslaved and sold to the Americas between 1492 and 1888 arrived after 1780 (p. 93). Slavery expanded alongside industrial capitalism.

3. Frontiers and externalization [pp. 93-95]

Guyanese production rose 862 percent in 1789-1802; Brazil became important in the 1790s. Cotton required “ceaseless spatial expansion.” If 1800 British consumption were domestic, Beckert estimates 416,081 acres and 90,360 workers; by 1860, 6.3 million acres (37 percent of arable land) and over 1 million workers. Overseas supply escaped domestic constraints.

4. Instability redirects supply [pp. 95-97]

Saint-Domingue supplied 24 percent of British imports before revolution and 4.5 percent by 1795. Haiti broke a key frontier; price spikes redirected attention to the United States.

Chapter 5 · pp. 98-135

Slavery Takes Command

Chapter thesisThe United States dominated because planters combined ecology with territorial conquest, indigenous dispossession, chattel slavery, innovation, infrastructure, finance, and political power.

1. Speed and technology [pp. 98-105]

South Carolina exports rose from under 10,000 pounds in 1790 to 6.4 million in 1800. U.S. output rose from 1.5 million pounds (1790) to 36.5 million (1800) and 167.5 million (1820); it led British supply by 1802. Whitney's 1793 gin reportedly multiplied upland ginning productivity by fifty. Mexican seeds brought by Walter Burling in 1806 could be picked three to four times faster than Georgia Green Seed.

2. Why the United States? Six-part answer [pp. 105-115]

  1. Land seizure: purchases, wars, coerced treaties, settler violence. Beckert's figure that 67 percent of 1850 cotton grew outside the 1800 U.S. is challenged by Olmstead-Rhode.
  2. Portable slave labor: declining Upper South tobacco supplied captives. About 250,000 were relocated in 1790-1820; the internal trade moved up to 1 million.
  3. Planter political power: constitutional representation, presidents, courts, Congress, states, militias, and federal removal.
  4. Infrastructure: rivers, steamboats after 1817, railroads, gins, presses. Transport reportedly added 3 percent to U.S. cost versus roughly 50 percent in India.
  5. Capital: factors and Europe advanced funds; enslaved people secured loans. Slave collateral appeared in 88 percent of Louisiana mortgage loans and 82 percent in South Carolina.
  6. Scale/control: gangs, records, equipment, experimentation. In 1860, 85 percent of cotton came from units over 100 acres; their owners held 91.2 percent of enslaved people.
直觉Natural endowment 只是入场券。美国把 land、labor、credit、transport、political power 做成可沿 frontier 复制的 package。

3. Violence and productivity [pp. 115-117]

Beckert foregrounds gang labor, quotas, account books, whipping, and near-total control. Following Edward Baptist, he says torture drove rising output. Olmstead-Rhode argue improved varieties explain much of higher picking rates and the “pushing system” evidence is mishandled.

4. Macro/global role [pp. 117-122]

Cotton was 32 percent of U.S. exports in 1820 and over half across 1815-60. A Mississippi plantation allegedly returned 22.5 percent in 1807; the price of a young adult male enslaved person in New Orleans rose from about $500 (1800) to as much as $1,800 before the Civil War. Manchester demand and finance intensified plantation growth; supply lowered textile prices.

5. Alternatives and contradiction [pp. 122-135]

India faced transport, local demand, subsistence priorities, merchant power, capital scarcity, and rational resistance to risky seeds and methods. Egypt used corvée, compulsory planting, state purchase, and monopoly sale under Muhammad Ali. Cheap slave cotton weakened household textile systems but dependence on U.S. slavery exposed Europe to rebellion, emancipation, and war.

Chapter 6 · pp. 136-174

Industrial Capitalism Takes Wing

Chapter thesisMechanized production spread fast but unevenly. Technology, capital, textile experience, and competition mattered; decisive was state capacity to protect markets, build infrastructure, mobilize labor, and sustain an industrial coalition.

1. Diffusion [pp. 136-142]

By 1860 Britain held 67.4 percent of mechanical spindles. German spindles rose from 22,000 (1800) to 2 million (1860); Catalonia nearly 800,000 (1861); United States 87,000 (1810) to 5 million (1860); Mexico had 58 mills, 25,000 spindles, and 2,600 looms (1843); Switzerland 1.35 million spindles (1857).

2. Preconditions [pp. 142-155]

  • Textile skills, merchants, putting-out networks, and accustomed labor.
  • Capital from textile profits or merchant fortunes.
  • Espionage, migrant artisans, copied machines, and engineers.
  • Pressure from cheap British imports.
  • Integrated domestic, foreign, or colonial markets.

The Boston Associates' Waltham mill began with $400,000; dividends exceeded 10 percent in many years and reached 17 percent in 1817. Families often had ties to slave and plantation commerce.

3. State capacity [pp. 155-161]

States subsidized firms, imported experts, excluded competition, removed internal tariffs, built transport, integrated markets, and policed borders. During Napoleon's blockade, Saxon spindles rose from 13,200 (1806) to seventeen times that by 1813. During the U.S. Embargo, spindles rose from 8,000 (1807) to 130,000 (1815); mills rose from 15 (1806) to 62 (1809) plus 25 under construction. Postwar tariffs institutionalized protection across the United States and Europe.

4. Comparative tests [pp. 161-173]

CaseOutcomeExplanation
New EnglandRapid growthSkills, merchant capital, copied technology, tariff, market, wage labor; slave cotton.
MexicoSignificant industryIndependent state, Banco de Avío, tariffs. Mills: 4 (1837) → 50+ (1847).
EgyptEarly surge, collapseCoercion built mills; weak wage institutions, fuel/management problems, imposed low tariffs.
Brazil / U.S. SouthWeak early industrySlave elites concentrated labor, capital, and policy on staple exports.
IndiaDeindustrialization, late takeoffColonial state denied protection despite skills, cotton, and demand.
YucatánFailureSmuggling, fragmented markets, weak borders, separatism, rebellion.

5. Synthesis [pp. 173-174]

Capitalism's dynamism came from coexisting regimes: frontier coercion produced inputs; state-centered factories mechanized output; commerce connected them. Industrial capitalism later developed alternatives to chattel slavery, but its formative growth depended on war capitalism.

NuanceBeckert concludes slavery and colonial exploitation were not eternally essential to capitalism. His claim is historical foundation and early expansion, not logical necessity in every later economy.

Critical reading

How to evaluate the argument

Strengths

  • Relational scale: factory, plantation, merchant, state, and consumer form one system.
  • Sequencing: network domination precedes technological domination.
  • Institutional pluralism: slavery and wage labor reinforce each other.
  • State formation: protection, fiscal power, infrastructure, and empire make markets.
  • Uneven development: colonial integration can block industrialization.

Vulnerabilities

  • Necessity vs. importance: centrality does not prove no free-labor counterfactual.
  • Aggregation: “cotton capitalists” can flatten conflicts among actors.
  • Top-down causality: coordination may be inferred from dispersed price responses.
  • Denominators: trade shares do not equal national-income contribution.
  • Technology/biology: seeds, gins, transport, and agronomy may explain more productivity.
阅读策略Beckert 的 system architecture 很强；遇到 “essential / indispensable / cheap labor” 要转到 Olmstead–Rhode 检查 denominator、counterfactual 与 source use。
Rapid review

Seminar-ready takeaways

  1. One-sentence thesis: Europe's cotton-led industrialization emerged from a global division of labor in which strong states and mechanized wage-labor factories depended on imperial markets, expropriated land, and coerced labor.
  2. Key concept: war capitalism reveals capitalism's violent prehistory beyond Europe's core.
  3. Key mechanism: network control generated inputs, markets, capital, knowledge, and state capacity; high wages induced mechanization.
  4. Comparative result: industry spread where capable states protected producers and mobilized markets/labor; colonial and slaveholding coalitions often blocked it.
  5. Best challenge: deep historical dependence is not automatically counterfactual necessity.

Discussion questions

  • Is “war capitalism” distinct from mercantilism?
  • What evidence would prove slavery necessary rather than highly important?
  • Does cheap cotton cause industrial growth, or does growth reorganize supply?
  • Can one model cover Britain, Mexico, Egypt, India, Brazil, and the U.S. South?
  • How should evidence of violence be balanced with productivity and macro data?
